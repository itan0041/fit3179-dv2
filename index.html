Gardening in Malaysia · 2014–2026

Grow Where You Are

In 2020, Malaysians searched for ways to grow food at home like never before. Eleven years of planting records show how little — and how much — actually changed in the ground beneath us.

One · The spike

The year everybody wanted to grow something

When the Movement Control Order began on 18 March 2020, Malaysians went looking for ways to feed themselves from a balcony, a porch or a patch of soil. Searches for hidroponik — hydroponics — nearly tripled from their 2014 level.

Then the interest drained away. By 2026 all three Malay-language search terms sit below where they stood in 2014: hidroponik down 18 per cent, berkebun down 18 per cent, and kebun sayur down 46 per cent. The enthusiasm was real, and it was temporary.

No agency in Malaysia counts gardening as a pastime. Nobody surveys the pots on a balcony or the beds behind a terrace house. What can be counted is the looking up, and these four curves are the only national signal this page has for gardening as a hobby. They measure attention rather than participation, and everything that follows keeps those two apart.

This rise and fall is the closest thing Malaysia has to a record of gardening as a pastime rather than a trade. No agency counts the people keeping chilli plants on a balcony, but almost all of them look up how to do it first — so what they searched for is the evidence this page starts from, and the rest of it asks what that enthusiasm did, and did not, change in the ground.

Two · The ground

Attention is not the same as acreage

It is tempting to read that spike as a gardening boom that reshaped the country. The planting records refuse to cooperate.

Malaysia's vegetable land had been shrinking for years: between 2014 and 2018 it lost 17.7 per cent of its area. The recovery began in 2019 — before the pandemic — and kept going long after the searching stopped, reaching 75,795 hectares in 2024, the highest in the eleven years on record.

These two things are not the same thing measured twice. The search data describes gardening as a pastime; the planting data counts commercial farms surveyed by the Department of Statistics. Neither counts the Malaysians who put a chilli plant on a balcony. Read together, though, they show a country whose food-growing capacity moved on a far slower rhythm than its enthusiasm did — and that gap is what the rest of this page is about.

Three · The map

Where Malaysia's vegetables actually grow

National totals hide an extraordinary concentration. More than a third of every vegetable hectare in Malaysia sits in one state.

Pahang holds 36 per cent of the country's vegetable land, most of it in the cool highland soils around Cameron Highlands.

Measured against its own population, Pahang works 16.5 hectares of vegetables for every 1,000 residents. Selangor, the most populous state, works 0.31.

Four · The mismatch

Searching for a garden, and having one, are different maps

Putting curiosity and cultivation on the same map shows where the two part company.

Interest runs highest in the north and east — Kelantan, Perlis and Terengganu — the states already closest to farming. But interest alone does not put food in the ground.

Perlis and Terengganu search as keenly as anywhere in Malaysia and grow very little. Meanwhile Kuala Lumpur and Selangor sit pale in both directions: they neither search for gardening nor grow much of anything.

Five · The shift

The growth went east. The losses went where the people are.

Over eleven years the map of Malaysian vegetable growing has quietly redrawn itself.

Kelantan more than doubled its vegetable land, up 156 per cent, and Perlis rose 112 per cent. The heaviest losses fell on the urban west coast: Selangor gave up 55 per cent of its vegetable land, and Pulau Pinang 27 per cent. Kuala Lumpur reports no commercial crop area at all.

Selangor and Kuala Lumpur together hold 27.8 per cent of Malaysia's population and 3.0 per cent of its vegetable land. That gap is the strongest argument this data makes for growing something at home: the people are furthest from the fields exactly where the fields have disappeared fastest.

For anyone in a state that lost its farmland, growing something at home becomes the practical response — which raises the last question: when to plant.

Six · The calendar

No spring, no autumn — here, the rain sets the calendar

Almost every gardening guide a Malaysian finds online was written for a country with four seasons. Ours has two, and they are wet and less wet.

Worldwide, searches for gardening swing hard with the northern spring: the busiest month runs more than twice as high as the quietest.

Malaysia's own curve is far flatter, and it does not follow the northern calendar at all. What it does follow is the rain — interest sits at its lowest in December, the single wettest month of the Malaysian year.

So when should you plant? Not on the calendar you read online. On the east coast and in much of Borneo the gentlest window is February to April, once the north-east monsoon has passed. In the north — Perlis, Kedah and Pulau Pinang — it falls earlier, January to March. Around Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and Perak the drier spell comes mid-year, between May and August.

How to read the numbers

Search interest is not a count of searches. Google scales each set of terms so that the busiest month equals 100. The search data on this page was retrieved in two separate queries, one for Malaysia and one worldwide, and each was scaled on its own.

Crop records are commercial. The Department of Statistics surveys farms and agricultural establishments. Home gardens are not counted, so this page never claims to measure household gardening directly. Search interest is the only national signal of gardening as a pastime, and this page reads it as a measure of attention, never as a count of how many people garden.

2026 is a partial year. Search data runs to September, so where years are compared, only January to September is used for every year.

Corrections and limits in the source data

Flowers are not measured in tonnes. The portal describes every production figure as metric tonnes, but the flower series reports stem counts in the hundreds of millions. Flower production is therefore shown as unavailable; flower area, in hectares, is reliable and is used.

Coconut sits inside industrial crops. The published methodology places coconut within industrial crops, so the nine categories overlap and must never be summed. Totals on this page use only the non-overlapping edible group.

One grid cell, two cities. NASA POWER works on a half-degree grid, so Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya return identical climate readings.
